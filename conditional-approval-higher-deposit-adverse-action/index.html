Approving a Tenant With a Bigger Deposit or a Co-Signer: Is It Adverse Action?

The report comes back with thin or bad credit. You don't want to say no, so you say yes, if the applicant pays a bigger deposit, finds a co-signer, or pays a few months up front. Applicants offer the same thing: "What if I pay six months in advance?" A conditional yes can still be adverse action under federal law, and state deposit caps limit how much extra you can ask for.

The short version. The CFPB says that if an applicant is "conditionally accepted" with adverse conditions, "such as requiring a co-signer or a larger deposit or a higher rent payment than other applicants," the landlord must give an adverse action notice naming the screening company it used [CFPB]. The duty applies when the decision is based "in whole or in part" on a consumer report [15 U.S.C. 1681m(a)]. Separately, California, Massachusetts and New York cap the deposit, so "pay more because of your credit" has a ceiling there [CA] [MA] [NY].

Why is a conditional approval adverse action?

The FCRA's definition of adverse action includes "an action taken or determination that is" made in connection with an application by a consumer and "adverse to the interests of the consumer" [15 U.S.C. 1681a(k)(1)(B)(iv)]. It isn't limited to a flat denial. The CFPB tells renters that landlords must tell them if they were "denied a lease, or charged a higher security deposit or other fee, because of information in your report" [CFPB].

Conditions the CFPB names [CFPB]:

  • Requiring a co-signer
  • Requiring a larger deposit than other applicants pay
  • Charging a higher rent than other applicants pay

The same CFPB page describes tough lease conditions after a negative report, such as "a requirement that you pay months of additional rent in advance or a higher security deposit" [CFPB].

Does it matter if the applicant offered to pay more?

The statute turns on whether you took the action "based in whole or in part on any information contained in a consumer report" [15 U.S.C. 1681m(a)]. Who suggested the terms isn't part of the test. If the report is part of why you'll only rent on tougher terms than you'd give other applicants, send the notice. If you require the same terms of every applicant regardless of what the report says, the report didn't drive the decision.

What must the notice say?

After an adverse action based on a consumer report, you must [15 U.S.C. 1681m(a)]:

  1. Give notice of the adverse action, orally, in writing or electronically.
  2. Disclose any numerical credit score you used, with the score information listed in 1681g(f)(1).
  3. Give the name, address and phone number of the consumer reporting agency that furnished the report, and a statement that the agency didn't make the decision and can't explain why it was made.
  4. Tell the applicant about their right to a free copy of the report from that agency, and their right to dispute its accuracy or completeness.

A simple routine for conditional approvals

  1. Write the condition into your screening criteria before you screen, for example "applicants below our credit standard may qualify with a co-signer."
  2. Check the deposit cap for your state before naming an amount.
  3. Send the adverse action notice when you offer the conditional terms, and keep a copy with the file.
