Calling a Previous Landlord: References, Rental Verification and the FCRA

A credit report tells you whether an applicant pays their bills. It usually doesn't tell you whether they paid rent, kept the place clean, or left on good terms. That's why many landlords still call the previous landlord. Here's how federal law treats that call when you make it yourself and when a company makes it for you, and which states write reference checks into their screening-fee rules.

The short version. Under the FCRA, a "consumer report" is information furnished by a consumer reporting agency; a report "containing information solely as to transactions or experiences between the consumer and the person making the report" is excluded [15 U.S.C. 1681a(d)(2)(A)(i)]. A former landlord describing their own tenancy with the applicant fits that description. If you pay a company that regularly gathers this kind of information, it is likely acting as a consumer reporting agency, and a report built from interviews about someone's "character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living" is an investigative consumer report with its own disclosure duty [1681a(e)] [1681d(a)]. California, Washington, Oregon and Minnesota all treat reference checks as part of screening [CA] [WA] [OR] [MN].

Is calling a previous landlord a consumer report?

The FCRA defines a consumer report as a communication "by a consumer reporting agency" bearing on a consumer's "credit worthiness, credit standing, credit capacity, character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living" that is used as a factor in deciding eligibility for credit, insurance, employment or another purpose the Act allows [1681a(d)(1)]. The same section then excludes "any report containing information solely as to transactions or experiences between the consumer and the person making the report" [1681a(d)(2)(A)(i)].

So when a former landlord tells you, from their own records, how long the applicant rented, what the rent was and whether it was paid on time, they are reporting their own experience with that tenant. What the statute adds is the definition of the agency: a "consumer reporting agency" is anyone who, for fees or on a cooperative nonprofit basis, "regularly engages in whole or in part in the practice of assembling or evaluating consumer credit information or other information on consumers for the purpose of furnishing consumer reports to third parties" [1681a(f)].

What changes when a company makes the calls for you?

If you hire a screening company to verify rental history, the company is assembling information about the applicant for a fee and passing it to you, which is the activity in the agency definition above [1681a(f)]. When that verification is done through personal interviews about the applicant's "character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living" with neighbors, friends, associates or others who know them, the report is an investigative consumer report [1681a(e)].

Before you order an investigative consumer report, the law requires you to tell the applicant in writing, no later than three days after you first request it, that such a report may be made, along with their right to ask about the nature and scope of the investigation; you also certify this to the agency [1681d(a)]. If the applicant asks in writing, you must disclose the nature and scope of the investigation within five days [1681d(b)]. And as with any consumer report, the agency may furnish it only for a permissible purpose, including "in accordance with the written instructions of the consumer to whom it relates" [1681b(a)(2)].

Which states mention reference checks in their screening rules?

StateWhat the statute says about referencesSource
CaliforniaThe screening fee covers "obtaining information about the applicant," which "may include, but is not limited to, personal reference checks and consumer credit reports." If the landlord doesn't do a personal reference check or get a credit report, the unused part of the fee must be returned[Civ. Code 1950.6(a), (e)]
WashingtonA landlord who screens on their own may charge actual costs, which "include costs incurred for long distance phone calls and for time spent calling landlords, employers, and financial institutions." The statutory adverse action notice has a box for "Information received from previous rental history or reference"[RCW 59.18.257(1)(b), (c)]
OregonScreening "includes but is not limited to checking references." Before taking a screening charge, the landlord's written notice must describe the process, including whether the landlord "contacts employers, landlords or other references"[ORS 90.295(1)(a), (3)(b)(C)]
MinnesotaIf the landlord doesn't perform "a personal reference check" or obtain a credit or tenant screening report, the unused part of the screening fee must be returned[Minn. Stat. 504B.173 subd. 2(b)]

For the refund side of these rules, see screening fee refunds when you don't screen. For the written criteria you may owe first, see written screening criteria by state.

Using what the reference tells you

If a bad reference leads you to deny an applicant or approve them on worse terms, check what notice you owe. Washington's statutory form requires you to say whether the decision was based on "previous rental history or reference" [RCW 59.18.257(1)(c)]; Oregon and Minnesota require written reasons or the unmet criteria after a denial (see adverse action notices and Oregon's screening rules).
