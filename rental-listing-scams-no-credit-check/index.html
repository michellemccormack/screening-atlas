Rental Listing Scams: "No Credit Check" Ads, $1 Credit-Check Links and Copied Listings

"No credit check, no background check, move in this week" sounds like a gift when you've been turned down twice. It's also how a lot of rental scams start. Below is what the Federal Trade Commission and two state consumer agencies say about how these scams work, how a real screening request differs, and what to do if your own listing has been copied.

The short version. Since 2020, people have reported nearly 65,000 rental scams to the FTC, with about $65 million in losses [FTC Data Spotlight, Dec. 2025]. Scammers pressure renters to pay before seeing the place, ask for screenshots of credit scores or send links to $1 "credit checks," and collect SSNs, license photos and pay stubs for identity theft [FTC]. The FTC says "legit landlords normally pull your credit score themselves – they don't tell you to get it for them" [FTC].

How common are rental listing scams?

  • Nearly 65,000 reports and about $65 million in reported losses since 2020; the FTC notes most scams are never reported, so this "likely reflects only a fraction of the actual harm" [FTC].
  • In the 12 months ending June 2025, about half of people who reported a rental scam said it started with a fake ad on Facebook, and 16% said it started on Craigslist [FTC].
  • People ages 18 to 29 were three times more likely than other adults to report losing money to a rental scam [FTC].
  • The median reported loss from January 2020 through June 2025 was $1,000 [FTC, note 1].

Is a "no credit check" rental always a scam?

No. Some private landlords do skip credit checks. The warning signs are what comes next: pressure to pay before a showing or a lease, an unusual payment method, or a request for your SSN and documents before you've agreed to rent [FTC] [Michigan AG]. Until you've agreed to rent a place, the FTC says, "a landlord doesn't need your Social Security number, credit score or other sensitive information" [FTC].

How does a real tenant screening request work?

On the big screening platforms, you authorize the check yourself, with the screening company, not by sending documents to the landlord:

  • TransUnion SmartMoveaffiliate link says the landlord's view doesn't include your Social Security number or account numbers, and the landlord can see your information for 30 days after you approve screening [SmartMove].
  • TurboTenant says landlords "cannot answer verification questions or authorize screening on their behalf"; the applicant completes identity verification [TurboTenant].

Some states cap screening fees, and some require the landlord to give you a copy of the report. See application fee caps by state and how to get a free copy of your screening report.

What can a landlord do when their listing is copied?

The FTC says scammers "make it harder for real landlords, many of whom report seeing their listings copied to defraud people" [FTC], and that warning signs posted inside homes on self-tour have "helped tip some people off" [FTC]. Practical steps:

  1. Put your real contact method, application link and fee in every listing, so a copy with different details stands out.
  2. Post a notice inside self-tour units that you never take payment by wire, gift card, crypto or payment app before a lease.
  3. Search your address now and then for copies with a different price or contact.
  4. Report copies to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov [FTC], and to the site that hosts the fake listing.

Where do renters report a rental scam?

The FTC takes reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and has recovery steps at ftc.gov/scams [FTC]. State consumer agencies, such as the Michigan Attorney General and the South Carolina Department of Consumer Affairs, also take complaints.
