Screening a Guarantor or Co-Signer: Fees, Consent and How Each Platform Handles It

When an applicant falls short on income or credit, the usual fix is a guarantor or co-signer. That raises its own screening questions: do you need the guarantor's consent, can you charge them a fee, does a fee cap apply to them, and how do you actually get their report on the platform you use? Here's what the statutes and the providers' own pages say.

The short version. A guarantor is a separate person with a separate report: on TurboTenant each co-signer files their own application and pays their own fee [TurboTenant]; RentRedi screens a guarantor through its "Screen Tenant" request [RentRedi]; SmartMove reports go out only after the invited person authenticates their identity [SmartMove]. In California, a guarantor or cosigner counts as an "applicant," so the screening-fee rules apply to them too [Civ. Code 1950.6(j)]. In Washington, requiring a guarantor is one of the outcomes listed on the statutory adverse action notice [RCW 59.18.257(1)(c)].

Consent: why the guarantor has to take part

Federal law lets a consumer reporting agency furnish a report "in accordance with the written instructions of the consumer to whom it relates" [15 U.S.C. 1681b(a)(2)]. The landlord platforms below are built around that kind of consent: the person being screened authorizes the report and verifies their identity themselves. TurboTenant, for example, says screening reports "are associated with individual applicants" [TurboTenant], so a guarantor who never logs in and authorizes will not produce a report.

Fees and fee caps for guarantors

California defines "applicant" for its screening-fee statute as anyone who asks to rent a unit "or an entity or individual who agrees to act as a guarantor or cosignor on a rental agreement" [1950.6(j)]. So the per-applicant cap, the itemized receipt, the refund of any unused amount and the right to a copy of the credit report all extend to a guarantor who pays a screening fee [1950.6(b), (d)-(f)]. Where a state caps the fee, compare a platform's per-person fee with the cap before you turn on applicant-paid screening; state caps are on our fee caps page.

Requiring a guarantor can be an adverse action

Washington's statutory adverse action notice lists "Approved with conditions" outcomes, including "Residency requires a qualified guarantor," and asks the landlord to mark whether the decision was based on a consumer report, insufficient credit information, rental history, or criminal, civil or employment records [RCW 59.18.257(1)(c)]. If a screening report is the reason you're asking for a guarantor, see what notice you owe on our adverse action notice page.
