Charged a Screening Fee but Never Screened: Refund Rules by State

A common complaint: an applicant pays a per-person "background check" fee, the unit goes to someone else, and the check was never run. Several states treat a screening fee as money for a specific purpose, so the part you didn't spend on screening goes back to the applicant. Here is what the statutes require, and what a landlord risks by keeping it.

The short version. California and Minnesota require returning any part of the fee not used, if no reference check or credit report was done [Cal. Civ. 1950.6(e)] [Minn. 504B.173 subd. 2(b)]. Oregon requires a refund within 30 days if the unit is filled before screening, or the applicant withdraws in writing before any screening was ordered; failing to refund can cost twice the fee plus $250 [ORS 90.295(5), (6)(b)]. Maryland requires returning non-deposit fees over $25, minus what was actually spent, within 15 days, for landlords with more than four units at one location [Real Prop. 8-213].

Can a landlord charge a fee when no unit is available?

Not in California, Oregon or Minnesota. California bars a screening fee when the landlord "know[s] or should have known that no rental unit is available at that time or will be available within a reasonable period of time" [1950.6(c)(1)]. Oregon has the same rule unless the applicant agrees otherwise in writing [ORS 90.295(4)], and Minnesota's is nearly identical [504B.173 subd. 1(1)]. Minnesota also bars the landlord from cashing or depositing a screening fee "until all prior applicants have either been screened and rejected, or offered the unit and declined" [subd. 1(3)].

Does the applicant get proof of what was spent?

In California, the landlord must give a receipt that itemizes "the out-of-pocket expenses and time spent" on the applicant [1950.6(d)]. In Oregon, the landlord must give a receipt for the charge and, after a screening by a screening company or credit bureau, "confirmation of the screening, including a copy of a receipt from the company or agency" [ORS 90.295(1)(a)]. Minnesota requires a written receipt on the applicant's request [504B.173 subd. 1(2)].

How do you avoid the problem as a landlord?

  1. Don't collect fees from more applicants than you can actually consider. In California, Oregon and Minnesota, charging when no unit is or will reasonably be available is barred (see above).
  2. Screen in order. Minnesota requires it before you cash a fee [subd. 1(3)], and one of California's two allowed fee processes considers completed applications in the order received [1950.6(c)(2)(A)].
  3. Or let the screening service charge the applicant directly, so no fee passes through you. Our cost calculator compares landlord-paid and applicant-paid setups.
  4. Keep the receipt from the screening company, since Oregon requires you to pass it on [ORS 90.295(1)(a)], and California requires an itemized receipt [1950.6(d)].
