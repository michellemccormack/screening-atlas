Is It Illegal to Run Credit or Background Checks in California? What State Law Actually Limits

You'll see it said online that credit or background checks are "illegal" for California rentals. They aren't. The state's own landlord-tenant guide says the landlord "may obtain your credit report from a credit reporting agency to assist the landlord with the screening process" and that "generally, a housing provider may check the criminal history of an applicant" [CA DRE guide, pp. 12, 16]. What California does is put rules around how you do it. Here they are, statewide.

The short version. You may charge an application screening fee only up to your actual out-of-pocket cost, capped at $30 per applicant adjusted for CPI since 1998 [Cal. Civ. 1950.6(b)], and only under one of two first-come or refund-everyone processes [1950.6(c)(2)]. If the applicant paid a fee, you must give them a copy of the credit report within seven days [1950.6(f)]. Criminal history checks are allowed, but not arrests without conviction, diversion, sealed or juvenile records, and not blanket bans [CA DRE guide, p. 12].

Can a California landlord run a credit check?

Yes. The information a landlord gathers with a screening fee "may include, but is not limited to, personal reference checks and consumer credit reports" [1950.6(a)]. A landlord "may, but is not required to, accept and rely upon a consumer credit report presented by an applicant" [1950.6(a)].

The exception is government rent subsidies. Where there is one, you can't use credit history without offering the applicant the option to provide "lawful, verifiable alternative evidence" of ability to pay their share, such as government benefit payments, pay records and bank statements, and you must reasonably consider it in place of credit history [Gov. 12955(o)(1)(B)]. You also can't use an income standard that isn't based on the tenant's portion of the rent [12955(o)(1)(A)]. You can still verify employment, call landlord references and verify identity [12955(o)(2)].

Can a California landlord check criminal history?

Generally yes, with limits. According to the state's 2026 guide [CA DRE guide, p. 12]:

  • You may not seek or consider arrests that didn't lead to a conviction, participation in a pretrial or post-trial diversion program, convictions sealed by a court, or convictions from the juvenile justice system.
  • Blanket bans on all applicants with criminal histories are prohibited.
  • A denial based on criminal history must rest on a "directly-related conviction": one with "a direct and specific negative bearing on a substantial, legitimate, and nondiscriminatory interest," such as the safety of residents, employees or the property.
  • Landlords should be able to give a copy of their criminal-history policy, offer a chance to present mitigating information, and wait to consider criminal history until financial and other qualifications are verified.

The guide cites California Code of Regulations, title 2, sections 12264 to 12271 for these rules [CA DRE guide, notes 28-31]. It also says local governments can't require landlords to run criminal background checks [CA DRE guide, pp. 12, 20].

What else can't a California landlord consider?

  • Source of income. Discrimination because of source of income, including housing vouchers, is unlawful, though you may ask about the level and source of income [Gov. 12955(a),(p)]. See Section 8 and source-of-income rules.
  • Combined income. Your income standard must count the aggregate income of people who will live together the same way it would for a married couple [12955(n)].
  • Immigration status. Landlords may not ask about immigration or citizenship status, but can request documents to verify identity and finances [CA DRE guide, p. 20]. See applicants without an SSN.
  • COVID-19 rental debt. Screening services and landlords can't use alleged COVID-19 rental debt as a negative factor [CA DRE guide, p. 16].
  • Masked evictions. When an eviction record is masked, credit reporting agencies can't include it in a tenant screening report [CA DRE guide, p. 16]. See eviction records and the laws that hide them.

What do you owe the applicant if you deny them?

If a denial is based in whole or part on a credit report or screening service, you must give written notice that says so, names the agency with its address and phone number, and tells the applicant they can get a free copy and dispute it [CA DRE guide, p. 16]. See adverse action notices.

Can an applicant use their own screening report?

Yes, if you choose to accept it. The state guide says landlords aren't required to accept a reusable tenant screening report, but if they do, they can't charge a screening fee or a fee to access it [CA DRE guide, p. 12]. See reusable screening reports by state.
