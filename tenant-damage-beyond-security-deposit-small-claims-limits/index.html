When Tenant Damage Is More Than the Security Deposit: Recovering the Rest, and Small Claims Limits by State

"$15,000 in property damage. Your security deposit is only $4,000." The deposit is the money the landlord already holds, not the most a tenant can owe. Deposit statutes generally leave the landlord's other claims intact, and the rest is collected the ordinary way: a demand, then a lawsuit, often in small claims court if the amount fits under the state's limit.

The short version. Several deposit statutes say outright that they don't stop a landlord from recovering other damages [A.R.S. 33-1321(F)] [Va. 55.1-1226(E)]. Small claims limits differ widely: $12,500 for an individual in California ($6,250 for a business) [CA Courts], $5,000 in Virginia [Va. 16.1-122.2], $20,000 in Minnesota conciliation court [Minn. 491A.01]. Above the limit, the claim goes to the regular civil court.

Does the security deposit cap what a tenant owes?

No, in the statutes reviewed here. Arizona's deposit section "does not preclude the landlord or tenant from recovering other damages to which the landlord or tenant may be entitled" [A.R.S. 33-1321(F)]. Virginia's has the same kind of sentence [Va. 55.1-1226(E)], and adds that if more damage is found after the deposit has been settled, the landlord may still recover it, though the tenant may use the move-out report as evidence the damage wasn't there at move-out [55.1-1226(G)]. Massachusetts says its deposit section doesn't limit a landlord's right to recover from a tenant who willfully or maliciously destroys or damages the property, when repair or replacement costs more than the deposit [c.186 s.15B(4)].

What happens to the deposit paperwork when damage exceeds it?

  • Virginia. When damage exceeds the deposit and needs a third-party contractor, the landlord must say so in writing within the 45-day period, and then gets 15 more days to itemize the damage and the repair cost [55.1-1226(E)].
  • Florida. A landlord who misses the 30-day notice forfeits the right to claim against the deposit, but may file an action for damages after returning it [Fla. Stat. 83.49(3)(a)]. In a lawsuit over the deposit, the prevailing party gets court costs and a reasonable attorney's fee [83.49(3)(c)].
  • Everywhere. Follow the deposit deadline first. A late or missing itemization can cost the landlord the deposit itself, and in some states a penalty, even when the damage was real. See deposit return deadlines by state.

What if the damage is above the small claims limit?

The claim goes to the state's regular civil court instead, with its own filing rules. Small claims limits cap what that court can hear or award; they don't cap what the tenant owes. Wisconsin's statute, for example, sets a separate, lower $5,000 limit for tort claims [Wis. 799.01(1)(cr)], so how a claim is framed can matter.

What helps a landlord win

  • A signed move-in checklist or report and dated photos. See move-in checklist requirements by state.
  • An on-time itemized statement with estimates, invoices or receipts.
  • Charges only for damage beyond ordinary wear and tear. See cleaning and repainting deductions.

An unpaid judgment or balance can also be reported to credit bureaus if the furnisher rules are followed. See reporting unpaid rent to credit bureaus.
