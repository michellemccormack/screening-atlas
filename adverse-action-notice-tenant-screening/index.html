Adverse Action Notices After a Tenant Screening Report: What Landlords Must Send

A rejected applicant often asks: "Do you have to tell me which company's report sank me?" Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, yes, if the report played any part in the decision. The rule is section 615(a) of the FCRA, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1681m(a) [15 U.S.C. 1681m]. Below is what it requires, in the statute's own terms, plus two states that add to it.

The short version. If you take an adverse action "based in whole or in part on any information contained in a consumer report," you must give the applicant notice of the action, the screening company's name, address and phone number, a statement that the company didn't make the decision and can't explain it, and notice of the applicant's rights to a free copy of the report within 60 days and to dispute it. If a numerical credit score was used, you must also disclose the score [1681m(a)].

When a notice is required

The duty applies to "any person" who takes "any adverse action with respect to any consumer that is based in whole or in part on any information contained in a consumer report" [1681m(a)]. "In part" matters: the report doesn't have to be the main reason.

For a rental application, the relevant definition of adverse action is "an action taken or determination that is (I) made in connection with an application that was made by ... any consumer ... and (II) adverse to the interests of the consumer" [1681a(k)(1)(B)(iv)]. In practice that covers more than a flat denial. Washington's statutory notice form, for example, lists approval on condition of an increased deposit, a qualified guarantor, last month's rent, or increased monthly rent as adverse actions [RCW 59.18.257(1)(c)].

DisputeThe right to dispute "the accuracy or completeness of any information" in the report with the agency [(a)(4)(B)]Oral, written or electronic

The 60 days run from when the applicant receives your notice: the agency must provide the disclosure free if the request comes "not later than 60 days after receipt by such consumer of a notification pursuant to section 1681m" [1681j(b)].

Oral notice is legal for most items, but the credit-score disclosure has to be written or electronic [(a)(2)], and a written notice gives you a record. SmartMove's own guidance says it's best to send the letter in writing and to keep a dated copy [SmartMove]. SmartMove also publishes an adverse action letter template on that page.

Who to name when the report came through a platform

The statute requires the name and contact details of the consumer reporting agency "that furnished the report to the person" [(a)(3)(A)]. If you ordered through a listing or rent-collection app, check the report itself for the agency's name and contact details; our report comparison notes which providers say their credit reports come from TransUnion.

A practical checklist

  1. Note which parts of the report, if any, affected your decision, including a decision to approve only with a co-signer or a larger deposit.
  2. Copy the reporting agency's name, address and phone number from the report [(a)(3)(A)].
  3. If you used a credit score, include the score and the related details [(a)(2)].
  4. Add the "agency didn't decide" statement and the free-copy (60 days) and dispute rights [(a)(3)(B), (a)(4)].
  5. In Washington, use the state form and state your reasons; in Oregon, send the written reasons within 14 days [WA] [OR].
  6. Send it in writing or electronically and keep a dated copy.
