Section 8 and Source-of-Income Rules by State: Vouchers, Income Minimums and Credit Checks

The federal Fair Housing Act doesn't list source of income as a protected class: it bars discrimination "because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national origin" [42 U.S.C. 3604(a)] (disability is covered in a separate subsection). Whether you can turn down a Section 8 voucher therefore depends on state and local law. Some states go further and tell you how to run an income test or a credit check for a voucher holder. Here are five, from the statutes.

The short version. In all five states below, refusing an applicant because they pay with a housing voucher or subsidy is barred. Washington makes you subtract the voucher from the rent before applying an income minimum [RCW 59.18.255(3)]. California requires an income standard based on the tenant's share of the rent, and lets a voucher holder offer other proof of ability to pay instead of credit history [Gov. Code 12955(o)]. Oregon allows a refusal for inability to pay only "taking into account the value of" the applicant's housing assistance [ORS 659A.421(2)(a)(B)].

How do you apply a "3x the rent" rule to a voucher holder?

In Washington, subtract the voucher first. Example: rent is $2,000 and the voucher pays $1,400, so a "3x the rent" rule is measured against the $600 the tenant pays [RCW 59.18.255(3)]. California reaches the same place by requiring the income standard to be "based on the portion of the rent to be paid by the tenant" [Gov. Code 12955(o)(1)(A)]. California also requires any income standard to count the combined income of people who will live together the same way it would for a married couple [12955(n)].

Can you still run a credit check on a voucher holder?

In California, yes, but only if you first offer the applicant the choice to give you other verifiable evidence that they can pay their share. If they choose that route, you must give them "reasonable time to respond" and "reasonably consider" that evidence in place of their credit history [Gov. Code 12955(o)(1)(B)]. The same subdivision says it doesn't limit your ability to verify employment, "request landlord references," or verify identity [12955(o)(2)]. Asking about the level or source of income is not, by itself, discrimination under California's definition [12955(p)(2)].

Can your ad say "no Section 8"?

Not in these states. Washington bars any ad or notice "that indicates a preference, limitation, or requirement based on any source of income" [RCW 59.18.255(2)]; Oregon and California have similar advertising bans [ORS 659A.421(2)(e)] [Gov. Code 12955(c)]. Minnesota's ban covers ads and "any form of application" that indicates discrimination based on a protected status [363A.09 subd. 1(3)].

Are there exceptions?

Washington lets a landlord refuse a voucher whose payments depend on the unit passing inspection, but only if the written repair estimate to pass is over $1,500 and the landlord hasn't received money from the state's landlord mitigation program to make the repairs [RCW 59.18.255(1)(a)]. A Washington violation can cost up to four and a half times the monthly rent, plus court costs and attorneys' fees [59.18.255(4)]. Oregon's definition excludes income "derived from a specific occupation or income derived in an illegal manner" [ORS 659A.421(1)(d)(B)].

Cities and counties can add their own rules on top of these. We haven't reviewed local ordinances on this page.
