How Long Should a Landlord Keep Tenant Screening Reports, and How to Destroy Them

After you pick a tenant, you're left with a pile of credit reports, background checks, pay stubs and bank statements from the applicants you didn't pick. How long should you keep them, and is tossing them in the recycling a problem? Federal law focuses on how you dispose of screening reports. A few states add their own disposal rules for documents with Social Security or account numbers.

The short version. If you hold a consumer report, or information taken from one, for a business purpose, you must take "reasonable measures to protect against unauthorized access to or use of the information in connection with its disposal" [16 CFR 682.3(a)]. Shredding, burning or pulverizing paper, and destroying or erasing electronic media, are the rule's examples [682.3(b)]. Screening platforms also limit how long you can view a report: 30 days on SmartMove and RentRedi, 90 days for credit and 180 for criminal reports on Rentec Direct [SmartMove] [RentRedi] [Rentec].

Does the FTC Disposal Rule apply to small landlords?

The rule applies to "any person over which the Federal Trade Commission has jurisdiction, that, for a business purpose, maintains or otherwise possesses consumer information" [16 CFR 682.2(b)]. "Consumer information" means any record about an individual, paper or electronic, "that is a consumer report or is derived from a consumer report," and compilations of those records [682.1(b)]. A tenant screening report is the consumer report; your notes or spreadsheet built from it are "derived from" it.

"Disposal" covers discarding or abandoning the information, and also selling, donating or transferring any medium it is stored on, "including computer equipment" [682.1(c)]. An old laptop with downloaded reports counts.

How do you properly destroy tenant screening reports?

The rule's examples of reasonable measures [16 CFR 682.3(b)]:

  • Burning, pulverizing or shredding papers so the information "cannot practicably be read or reconstructed."
  • Destroying or erasing electronic media so the information cannot practicably be read or reconstructed.
  • Hiring a record-destruction company after due diligence, such as reviewing an independent audit, checking references, or requiring certification by a recognized trade association.

The examples are "illustrative only," not an exclusive list [682.3(b)].

Is there a reason to keep screening records for a while?

The Disposal Rule says how to dispose, not when. One date to know: a lawsuit under the Fair Credit Reporting Act must be brought by the earlier of 2 years after the plaintiff discovers the violation or 5 years after the violation occurs [15 U.S.C. 1681p]. If you want proof on hand for that whole period, keep the records that show you followed the rules: the applicant's authorization, the adverse action notice you sent, and your written screening criteria. Keep them secured, and dispose of them under the rule when you're done.

A simple retention routine

  1. Download each report you rely on before the platform's viewing window closes, and store it in one secured place.
  2. Don't print reports or copy ID, SSN or bank account numbers into spreadsheets unless you need to.
  3. Keep your criteria, the applicant's authorization and any adverse action notice together with the decision.
  4. When you no longer need the file, shred paper and delete or wipe electronic copies, including on old computers and phones you sell or give away [16 CFR 682.1(c), 682.3(b)].
